# AvePoint — SaaS Churn Analysis

Churn case study using the RavenStack synthetic SaaS dataset, created by
**River @ Rivalytics** and published on Kaggle as
[SaaS Subscription & Churn Analytics](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/rivalytics/saas-subscription-and-churn-analytics-dataset).
The data is fully synthetic and contains no PII; it is used here under its
MIT-like licence, which requires crediting the author.

Covers the prediction problem (notebooks 01–10) and the three questions the
product team actually asked — why, predict, act (notebooks 11–16, summarised in
`docs/PRODUCT_QUESTIONS.md`).

## The short version

A SaaS company wants to know why customers churn and predict it early enough to
intervene. This builds the standard temporal churn formulation and reports what
the data supports rather than what the brief hoped for:

> **Nothing in this extract predicts churn, and the one pattern that looked
> like a cause is manufactured by the file.** Cross-sectional prediction sits at
> chance (nested CV **0.534 ± 0.016**, Cox concordance **0.509**). The churn
> hazard appears to rise **2.8× per year** (p = 2e-16) — and a simulation
> containing nothing but a uniform random number reproduces it at **2.78×**,
> with all 24 months inside the null band.

The rise is right-truncation. No churn date can land after the last day of the
extract, and every churn date in this file is a uniform draw between signup and
that boundary (KS p = **0.92**, and inside every signup quarter). A uniform draw
on a shrinking remaining window has hazard `1/(END − t)`, which climbs without
limit toward the end of the file on data where nothing happened. Redrawing the
dates from that rule alone gives a rate ratio of **1.0885** against the observed
**1.0893**, putting the observed effect at the **52nd percentile of pure noise**.
`src/generator.py`, `notebooks/16_generator_audit.py`.

The p-value is the part worth dwelling on: the null rule's *median* trend
p-value is **2.8e-16**, marginally more significant than the real data. A small
p-value is evidence against one null, and the null being tested here ("the rate
is flat") was never the one that mattered.

The prediction negative differs in strength by source, and the deck says so.
For product usage and support it is flat — those timestamps correlate with their
own account's signup date at **r = 0.002** and **r = 0.014**, so there was never
anything to find. For customer attributes it is an underpowered null: a planted
but genuinely weak relationship scores about the same here, so with 54 positives
and a self-contradicting label — three definitions of "churn" agree on **20%** of
accounts — "we could not detect it" is the defensible claim.

Every number here is regenerated by the notebooks — see [docs/RUNNING.md](docs/RUNNING.md).

## The three product questions

The brief asks for three things. `docs/PRODUCT_QUESTIONS.md` answers them in
full; the summary is:

| Question | Answer | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Why are users leaving?** | **This extract cannot say.** No attribute, segment, tenure band or stated reason explains who leaves, and the apparent ×2.8/yr calendar rise is reproduced by a random-date simulation. | 11, 12, 13, **16** |
| **How to predict churn before it happens?** | **Not from this data.** Three independent formulations land at chance. Demanding 30 days of lead time drops every horizon to chance or below. | 03, 04, 12 |
| **What actions improve retention?** | **Not identifiable** — no intervention is recorded anywhere in the schema. Separately, break-even churn probability is **10.3%** against a base rate of **30.5%**, so targeting is unnecessary. | 14, 15 |

Five results from that work are worth pulling out, because each one reverses
something a competent analysis would otherwise have shipped:

1. **The strongest result in the study is an artefact, including its p-value.**
   Churn accelerating ×2.8/yr at p = 2e-16 is reproduced, effect size and
   significance alike, by drawing each churn date uniformly between signup and
   the last day of the file. It was the only positive finding here and the only
   one that had not been tested against a null that imitates the generator.
   **This reverses "find out what changed in 2024"**, which would have sent a
   team after a pricing event that did not happen.
2. **The stated churn reasons are noise.** Uniform over six codes (p = 0.70),
   unrelated to nine behavioural measures (min BH p = 0.91), and independent of
   the customer's own free text (Cramér's V = 0.09). A "top churn reasons" chart
   from this field would be confident and fictional.
3. **Churn is not front-loaded, and the obvious analysis says it is.** The
   pooled hazard falls at ρ = 0.737, p = 1.7e-13 — but within every signup
   cohort ρ returns to 1. **This reverses the onboarding recommendation** this
   project previously made. The reason is the same right-truncation as (1):
   simulated data with no tenure effect in it clears p < 0.05 in **93%** of runs,
   and more strongly than the real data does (p = 0.002 against 0.006).
4. **SHAP produces a clean driver chart from a chance-level model.** The top
   feature sits inside the shuffled-label distribution (p = 0.24), twelve
   different features win across 25 bootstrap resamples, and permutation
   importance on held-out data is *beaten* by noise (p = 1.0).
5. **The cost-benefit arithmetic should have come first.** One division shows
   treating everyone is already profitable — before the modelling effort that
   established the model does not work.

## Key findings

1. **The label had to be redefined.** `churn_flag` is undated, and it is
   statistically unrelated to the event log rather than just noisy — 37.6%
   agreement against 38.6% expected by chance, κ = −0.016. Found by counting,
   not modelling.
2. **Selection is worth more than the model.** Choosing the best of ten rungs
   costs 0.049 AUC, most of the apparent signal, and eight of ten rungs win at
   least once across 25 outer folds.
3. **The signal does not survive a lead-time requirement.** Demand 30 days of
   warning and every horizon drops to chance or below.
4. **Post-outcome columns are worth +0.37 AUC**, landing at a plausible-looking
   0.79 rather than an obviously broken 0.99.
5. **More feature engineering did not help; more rows did.** 21 extra features
   moved the score slightly down, while pooling four cutoffs cut measurement
   noise by 65%.
6. **The one large effect is calendar time, not customers.** Hazard ×2.8/yr on a
   flat at-risk base, p = 2e-16 — while Cox over 21 baseline covariates returns
   a global p of 0.57.
7. **A falling hazard with tenure was composition, not onboarding.** ρ = 0.737
   pooled (p = 1.7e-13), ρ ≈ 1 within every cohort.
8. **Explainability tooling has no failure mode.** SHAP ranked a chance-level
   model cleanly; five checks against nulls were needed to show the ranking was
   an artefact.
9. **The economics moot the ranking problem.** Break-even 10.3% against a 30.5%
   base rate — one division, and it belongs at the start of a churn project.

## What I would tell the business

**Do not deploy this, and do not rank a CSM call list on it.** The ladder maximum
does not clear chance at the lower bound of its interval, and once model selection
is cross-validated the estimate sits at chance. A ranking built on noise is worse
than no ranking, because it consumes CSM time and creates false confidence.

**You also do not need one.** Break-even churn probability at these economics is
10.3% and the cohort base rate is 30.5%, so contacting every at-risk account is
already profitable and no ranking improves on a policy that is correct for
everyone (notebook 15). Net campaign value at the model's best threshold is
$53,000 against $52,400 for treat-everyone — a 1% gain, itself optimistic.

The useful output of this work is the diagnosis, and it is actionable, in order
of leverage:

1. **Get an export where every event carries the customer it happened to.**
   Usage and ticket dates are currently uniform over the whole extract and
   unrelated to their own account (r = 0.002, r = 0.014), which is why 19,128 of
   24,979 usage rows predate their own subscription. This is not a column to
   repair — the timestamps have to be collected against the right accounts. It
   is first because it is the only thing that unblocks questions 1 and 2.
2. **Fix the label definition.** Three sources disagree; until they agree on what
   churn *is*, no modelling effort is recoverable.
3. **Instrument interventions.** No CSM touch, discount or campaign appears
   anywhere in the schema, which is why question 3 is unanswerable at any sample
   size rather than merely underpowered. This one is independent of the other
   two and can start immediately.
4. **Then, and only then, ask for the business timeline** — pricing changes,
   release history, competitor events. It is the right request for question 1
   and it is worth nothing against the current export, because it would join real
   events onto dates that were assigned at random.
5. **Then re-measure.** The pipeline is parameterised by cutoff, so that is one
   command — and use the pooled rolling-origin design, which already gives three
   times the labelled rows.

**Do not commission an investigation into what changed in 2024.** That
recommendation stood here until `notebooks/16_generator_audit.py`, and it rested
on the single most significant number in the project. It would have sent a team
looking for a pricing event, outage or competitor move that did not happen.

**And do not build the structured onboarding programme.** That recommendation
appears in notebook 05, resting on `days_since_signup` being the top single
feature and on a tenure-band table. Notebook 12 shows both are a composition
artefact: within any signup cohort the hazard is flat, so a day-300 account is
as likely to leave as a day-10 account. Notebook 16 gives the cause underneath
the composition — accounts that signed up recently have a shorter window for a
random churn date to land in, so simulated data with no tenure effect at all
reproduces the gradient in 93% of runs. Both earlier recommendations are left in
place rather than quietly deleted, with each reversal recorded where it was
found.

## Problem framing

`accounts.churn_flag` is a static flag with no date attached — it cannot be
placed relative to any cutoff. It is also **unrelated to** the `churn_events`
log rather than merely inconsistent with it: the two agree for 188 of 500
accounts (37.6%) against **38.6% expected if they had nothing to do with each
other**, κ = −0.016, χ² p = 0.56.

The distinction matters, because raw agreement invites the wrong rebuttal. The
flag fires for 22% of accounts and the event log for 70.4%; two unrelated
columns with rates that far apart already agree ~39% of the time, so 37.6% is
not "worse than chance" and inverting the flag does not recover a signal
(62.4% observed vs 61.4% expected, κ = +0.025). It is not a usable target in
either direction.

The modelled target is the standard churn formulation instead:

> Given everything observable on **2024-06-30**, will this account churn within
> the next **90 days**?

```
|<-------- observation -------->|<---- prediction ---->|
2023-01-01                 2024-06-30            2024-09-28
       features built here             label defined here
```

- **Eligible**: signed up before the cutoff, holding a subscription still open
  at the cutoff, and not already churned → **177 accounts**.
- **Label**: first churn event within 90 days → **54 positives (30.5%)**. The
  window is inclusive at both ends so it matches the eligibility rule exactly.
- **Features**: only from rows dated before the cutoff, with fields that
  *resolve* after it censored.

90 days is the usual operational horizon for SaaS churn, chosen before the
sweep below rather than picked from it.

### The cohort rule is the weakest part of the framing

Eligibility comes from the **subscriptions** table, the label from
**churn_events**, and those two agree on only 58% of accounts. The funnel makes
the consequence concrete:

```
500  accounts
347  signed up before the cutoff
335  ...holding a live subscription
177  ...with no prior churn event   <- the cohort
```

**158 accounts, 47% of the eligible pool, are dropped for having a churn event
before the cutoff while still holding an open subscription.** The exclusion is
defensible under the chosen label, but it does mean the label's own incoherence
selects the study population. `notebooks/10_sanity_checks.py` scores three
alternative cohort definitions and none rescues the result, so this is
documented rather than changed.

## Results

Model ladder, repeated stratified CV (5 folds × 10 repeats, identical folds):

| Rung | Model | CV ROC-AUC | 95% CI |
|------|-------|-----------|--------|
| 0 | Prior (no features) | 0.500 | — |
| 1 | Decision stump | 0.516 | [0.37, 0.64] |
| **2** | **Logistic (L2, C=1)** | **0.583** | **[0.37, 0.75]** |
| 3 | Logistic (L2, C=0.05) | 0.565 | [0.40, 0.77] |
| 4 | Logistic (L1, C=0.1) | 0.536 | [0.39, 0.69] |
| 5 | Random forest (depth 4) | 0.550 | [0.41, 0.74] |
| 6 | LightGBM (pipelined) | 0.543 | [0.40, 0.73] |
| 7 | LightGBM (native NaN + categoricals) | 0.544 | [0.38, 0.77] |
| 8 | XGBoost (native NaN + categoricals) | 0.569 | [0.43, 0.74] |
| 9 | HistGradientBoosting (native NaN) | 0.552 | [0.33, 0.73] |

**No rung clears chance at the lower bound of its interval.** The notebook says
so explicitly rather than ranking regardless — the "winner" here is the winner of
a coin-flipping contest.

**Giving the boosters a fair shot.** Routing LightGBM through the same
`SimpleImputer` + `OneHotEncoder` as the linear models handicaps it: gradient
boosters learn a split direction for missingness rather than needing it filled,
and take native categorical splits rather than a one-hot expansion. Rung 7 passes
raw `NaN` and pandas `category` dtype straight through. A 54-point grid search
adds a little more, to **0.563** re-scored on the ladder's folds, and still does
not beat a plain regularised linear model.

One methodological trap, which did not behave the way the textbook says.
`GridSearchCV.best_score_` for that tuned model reads **0.527** against an
independent re-score of **0.563** — lower, not higher. The two differ in
resampling scheme (one 5-fold split vs 5×10) as well as in selection, and at 177
rows the scheme is worth more than the selection. The surviving rule is the
stronger one: **`best_score_` is not comparable to another model's CV score in
either direction.** Re-score on shared folds or do not compare.

### The number to quote: nested CV

The ladder reports each rung honestly. Quoting the winner does not: choosing the
maximum of ten candidates is itself a fitting step. Nested CV moves selection
inside each outer fold, repeated over 5 outer splits because a single split
moves by ~0.09 AUC on the seed alone.

The whole thing is one `cross_validate` call over a `GridSearchCV` whose grid is
the ladder — making selection an estimator is what lets sklearn cross-validate
it (`model.ladder_search`).

| | ROC-AUC |
|---|---:|
| Ladder maximum (optimistic) | 0.583 |
| **Nested CV (honest)** | **0.534 ± 0.016** |
| Chance | 0.500 |
| Cost of selecting the winner | **0.049** |

Selection was worth 0.049 AUC, most of the apparent signal. Across 25 outer
folds **eight of the ten rungs won at least once**. A genuinely better model
wins consistently; eight different winners is what selecting on noise looks
like.

The permutation test on the fixed model gives p = 0.076, but it holds the model
constant and so does not account for having chosen it. The nested figure does.

**Operating point.** Threshold favours recall, since a missed churner costs more
than a wasted outreach call:

| | Threshold chosen in-sample | Threshold chosen on a held-out half |
|---|---|---|
| Recall | 0.722 | **0.748** |
| Precision | 0.368 | **0.329** |
| F1 | 0.497 | **0.448** |

The predictions are out-of-fold but the threshold is picked by maximising F1 on
those same predictions, worth **+0.049 F1** of optimism. Small, and it is the
ladder's selection effect one level down, so it gets measured rather than
asserted away.

## Honest limitations

- 177 accounts, 54 positives, 0.74 events per variable. Every interval is wide.
- Short horizons are underpowered. 23 positives at 30 days cannot detect a
  modest effect, so that row reads "cannot tell", not "no signal".
- The headline is still a single cutoff. The pooled design in `08` should be the
  default in a production evaluation, with proper rolling-origin backtesting.
- The reported operating point is +0.049 F1 optimistic from in-sample threshold
  selection.
- Source data has integrity problems: 1,077 of 2,000 tickets predate their
  account's signup; 19,128 of 24,979 usage rows predate their subscription's
  start; `satisfaction_score` is documented 1–5 but only ever takes 3, 4 or 5.
  Surfaced by `clean.integrity_report`, not silently repaired.
